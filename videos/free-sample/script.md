## Fact notes

- **Example figures:** the $4.50 pastry, its $1.20 food cost and 100 sold in a week are an example cafe, framed with "Say a new pastry is…".
- **20% off gives away $90:** 20% of $4.50 = $0.90 a pastry; × 100 sold = $90. The discount goes to every buyer, including the ones who would have paid full price.
- **Tastes cost about $12:** 10 pastries at a $1.20 food cost = $12, cut into four = 40 tastes. A food cost near 25 to 30% of the price is a common bakery target ($1.20 / $4.50 = 27%).
- **"A free taste usually sells more":** sampling works through trying before buying (it removes the risk of ordering something unknown) and reciprocity, the pull to give back after receiving something free (Robert Cialdini, *Influence*, the chapter on reciprocity). Retail sampling studies report sizable lifts in sales of the sampled product (for example the in-store sampling coverage in The Atlantic, "The Psychology of the Free Sample", 2014). The line says "usually", not a figure; the video's own math compares cost, not a conversion rate.
- **Guests learn to wait for the deal:** reference-price effect: repeated discounts lower the price shoppers expect to pay and train them to buy on promotion (a long-standing finding in pricing research, e.g. Kalwani and Yim, Journal of Marketing Research, 1992). Said as a general tendency, not a statistic.
- **Hand them out yourself:** offering a taste in person, with the item's name, makes the link to the item on the menu and uses reciprocity; a tray left out gets grazed without the link.

## Post caption

Everyone reaches for a discount when a new item isn't selling. A free taste usually sells more. Say a new pastry is $4.50. Twenty percent off, on a hundred sold in a week, gives away $90, mostly to people who would have paid full price. Ten pastries cut into tastes cost about $12, and the price stays whole. Sample the new or pricey item, keep the bites small and by the register, and hand them out yourself, with the name.
